## Should I use a freelancer or an agency for my website?

Hire an agency when the work has to run in several directions at once or must
survive one person leaving. Hire one specialist when the work is a single
site, a single strategy, and you would rather talk to the person who is
actually doing it.

That is the short version, and for most small and mid-sized companies the
second case is the common one. The rest of this page is the long version:
what each side genuinely gives you, what each costs, and the questions that
tell you which one you are actually dealing with.

I am the second kind — one developer who builds and optimises sites — so take
the comparison for what it is and check the reasoning rather than the source.
The parts where an agency wins are set out as plainly as the parts where it
does not.

## What an agency sells beyond the work itself

An agency is not mainly selling skill. Skill is distributed fairly evenly
across both sides. An agency sells three things that a single person cannot
manufacture:

**Capacity in parallel.** Five people can run design, front-end, content and
advertising at the same time. One person runs them in sequence. If your
deadline is fixed and the scope is wide, sequence is a real problem.

**Continuity.** If the person on your project leaves, gets ill or is moved,
an agency replaces them and the work continues. With one specialist, illness
is a stopped project. This is the single strongest argument for an agency and
it is rarely stated this plainly in agency marketing, which prefers to talk
about process.

**Procurement compliance.** Large companies and public bodies often require a
vendor with liability insurance, a legal department, a certain turnover, or
simply a company rather than a sole trader. That is not snobbery, it is their
purchasing policy, and no amount of quality on your side changes it.

If any of those three is your actual constraint, stop reading comparisons.
You need an agency.

## What one specialist sells that an agency cannot

The things that come back in the other direction are less visible but they
decide how the project feels.

**The person deciding is the person doing.** In an agency the brief passes
from a salesperson to an account manager to a designer to a developer. Every
handover loses something, and what it loses is usually the reason behind a
decision. When one person holds the whole thing, the reason never has to be
re-explained.

**No management overhead in the price.** An agency's rate carries the account
manager, the sales process, the office and the people between you and the
work. On a small site that overhead can be a large share of the invoice.

**Direct, undelayed answers.** You ask on Monday and get the answer from the
person who will implement it, not a reply saying the question was passed to
the team.

**One head holding development and search together.** This matters more than
it sounds. Most sites lose rankings during a redesign because the people who
built the new one never saw the old one's search data. When the same person
does both, that failure mode disappears.

## When an agency is the right answer

Be realistic about scale. An agency is right when:

- several workstreams must run simultaneously against a hard date;
- the site is one part of a campaign that also involves paid media, PR and
  video production;
- your company's purchasing rules exclude sole traders;
- you need guaranteed cover during holidays and illness, written into a
  contract with penalties;
- the project is large enough that a dedicated project manager genuinely
  saves you time rather than adding a layer.

## When one specialist is the right answer

One specialist is right when:

- you are building or rebuilding one site rather than running a campaign;
- the decisions are judgement calls that benefit from context, not tasks to
  be distributed;
- you want development and search handled as one job, not two suppliers
  blaming each other;
- your budget would otherwise buy a junior at an agency instead of a senior
  working alone;
- you care about reaching the person who did the work six months later.

## Is a freelancer cheaper than an agency, and what does each actually cost?

Usually yes, and for a reason that has nothing to do with quality: you are
not paying for the layer between you and the work.

Published rates vary so widely that averages are useless, so here are mine, in
euros, as a solo developer invoicing from Poland: a company website from
€2,000 with design included, a landing page €700–1,400, a redesign that keeps
existing rankings from €1,000, an SEO audit €250, ongoing search work €800 a
month. A comparable agency quote for the same brief is commonly two to four
times that, and part of that difference is real — it buys the capacity and
continuity described above.

What should worry you is not a high price but an unexplained one. Ask either
side what changes the number. If the answer is a list of concrete things —
number of languages, number of templates, whether content is ready, whether
a catalogue or a client area is involved — the estimate is grounded. If the
answer is a round figure with no structure behind it, the risk is yours.

## Freelancer or agency for a B2B website redesign

A redesign is the case where the choice matters most, because a redesign can
lose you traffic that took years to earn. The failure is almost always
organisational rather than technical: the new site is designed by people who
never looked at which pages brought enquiries, so those pages are rewritten,
merged or dropped, and their addresses change without redirects.

Ask both candidates the same question: what will you do with the pages that
currently bring us enquiries? The useful answer names the data source,
describes mapping every existing address to a new one, and treats the pages
that already work as something to preserve rather than redecorate. The answer
to avoid is any version of "we will make it modern". How I handle this is on
the [website redesign](/services/website-redesign-services) page.

## The contract: who owns the code, the domain and the access

This section is the same for both sides, and it is where most of the real risk
sits.

Your contract should say, in writing, that you own the code, the domain, the
hosting account, the content and every credential, and that they are handed
over at the end of the project without a separate negotiation. Ask where the
domain is registered and in whose name. A surprising number of disputes are
not about quality at all; they are about a supplier holding an access as
leverage.

Then ask the uncomfortable question of a solo specialist: what happens if you
disappear? A reasonable answer is specific — the code is in a repository you
control, hosting and the domain are in your accounts rather than mine,
documentation is in the repository, and any other developer can pick it up.
If the answer is reassurance rather than mechanics, that is your signal.

## What to check before you hire one specialist

- **Look at live sites, not screenshots.** Open them on a phone. Check that
  they load, that forms submit, that they are still online. Mine are in the
  [portfolio](/portfolio).
- **Ask what they would not take on.** Anyone who takes on everything is
  either very large or not being straight with you.
- **Ask for the reasoning behind one decision in their portfolio.** Specifics
  cannot be faked; adjectives can.
- **Check how they answer before you pay.** Response time and clarity during
  the quote are an accurate preview of the project.
- **Confirm the handover terms in writing**, as above, before any money moves.

Those same five checks work on an agency. The difference is that with an
agency you should also ask who specifically will do the work, and whether
that person is available for the whole project — the people in the pitch are
not always the people on the job.

## Common questions about hiring a freelancer or an agency

**What is the difference between a freelancer and a web agency?**
An agency distributes your project across several people and charges for the
coordination; a solo specialist holds the whole project and charges for the
work. The practical difference you feel is handovers: an agency has them
internally, a solo specialist does not.

**How do agency services compare to online freelance marketplaces?**
They are three different things, not two. A marketplace sells you a transaction
with a stranger, mediated by escrow and ratings; an agency sells you a managed
team; an independent professional with their own practice and name sells you
accountability, because their next client comes from this one. Price order is
usually marketplace, then independent, then agency — but the marketplace price
excludes the time you will spend managing it.

**Is a freelancer cheaper than an agency?**
Usually, because the rate does not carry sales, account management and office
costs. The gap is largest on small projects, where agency overhead is a big
share of a small invoice, and smallest on large ones, where you are paying for
capacity you actually need.

**How much does a freelance web developer charge?**
Rates vary too widely for an average to mean anything. Mine, as a reference
point: a company website from €2,000 with design included, a landing page
€700–1,400, a redesign from €1,000, an SEO audit €250, ongoing search work
€800 a month. Ask any candidate what moves the number — the structure of the
answer tells you more than the figure.

**What happens if my freelancer disappears mid-project?**
That is the real risk of hiring one person, and the way to manage it is
structural rather than emotional: the repository, the domain and the hosting
must be in your accounts from day one, not handed over at the end. Then the
worst case is a delay while you find someone else, not a lost project.

**Can one person really do both development and SEO?**
For a company site, yes, and it removes the most common cause of traffic loss
after a redesign — the new site being built by people who never saw the old
one's search data. For very large sites with separate content, engineering and
analytics teams, no: there the work is genuinely several jobs.

**Who owns the website when the project ends?**
You should, and it should say so in the contract: code, domain, hosting
account, content and every credential. Ask where the domain is registered and
in whose name before you pay anything.

**Can a solo developer work with a company in another country?**
Yes, and the practical questions are administrative rather than technical:
whether they can issue a compliant invoice in your currency, how VAT is
handled between your countries, and how many working hours your time zones
share. I invoice in euros from Poland and work in English, Polish and Russian.

**We are a large company with a procurement policy. Should we even consider a solo specialist?**
Check the policy first. If it requires a company of a certain size, liability
insurance or a vendor on an approved list, the decision is already made and
comparing quality is wasted effort.

## How to decide between an agency and one specialist in one sitting

Write down the three things your project actually needs. If two of them are
capacity, cover and compliance, hire an agency and stop comparing. If two of
them are judgement, continuity of context and a direct line to the person
doing the work, hire one specialist.

Then ask both candidates the same four questions: what changes your estimate,
what will you do with the pages that already bring enquiries, what exactly do
we own at the end, and who specifically does the work. The answers will
separate the two faster than any portfolio.
