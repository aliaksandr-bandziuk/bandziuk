## Title

Your next client asks an AI before they ask Google. I built a consultant's website for that

## Subtitle

38 of 40 searches in one field now end with an AI answer. Here is what that changed in how the site was planned, written and built.

---

Most consultant websites are built for the person who has already decided to get in touch. There is a services page, a biography and a contact form. That works — for the one visitor in twenty who arrives ready to write.

The other nineteen are still working out their problem. They are not looking for a consultant yet. They are looking for an answer.

I recently built a website for a retail and fashion analytics consultant — someone who works on assortment, pricing, product data and reporting — and the whole project was designed around those nineteen people. This is what that meant in practice.

## I looked at the search results before designing anything

Before drawing a single layout, I checked what people actually ask about this field, in English, Polish and Russian, and what they see when they ask it. Four findings came out of it, and each one changed the project.

**An AI answer appeared on almost every question.** Of 40 English queries, 38 showed a ready-made AI answer at the top of the page. In Polish it was 35 of 37. In Russian, all 36. The contest is no longer for a place in a list of links. It is for being the source that the answer quotes.

**Demand for the service itself was tiny.** Searches about assortment planning run to single and double figures a month in all three languages. A site built only around them would have had no visitors at all.

**The real volume was in the problems.** Far more people search for how to calculate a margin, a sell-through rate or stock turnover than for an analytics consultant. They are trying to solve it themselves first.

**The people who do the work were missing from the results.** The top places belonged to agencies, software vendors and general reference sites — people who write about the work rather than doing it. That gap became the site's position.

## A tool, not a brochure

The result is [tatsianabandziuk.com](https://www.tatsianabandziuk.com): 153 pages in English, Polish and Russian, each language written for its own searches rather than translated from the others.

Instead of describing expertise, the site uses it:

- **Five retail calculators** — margin and markup, sell-through, stock turnover, GMROI and open-to-buy. Each one is a full page with the calculation, the formulas, a worked example and the common mistakes.
- **Downloadable Excel templates**, with every formula checked automatically, because a template that is wrong by one cell is worse than none.
- **54 articles and 18 case studies**, each starting with the direct answer in its first sentence or two.
- **572 questions answered in plain text** across the three languages — because the questions people type into a search box are the same questions they now ask an AI assistant.

The design speaks the language of the consultant's clients. They spend their days in spreadsheets and reporting tools, so the site does too: the contact form looks and behaves like a spreadsheet, select numbers in a table and their sum appears at the bottom as it would in Excel, and the case studies filter like a dashboard, highlighting the matching work instead of hiding the rest. Someone who works with these tools recognises the specialism before reading a word about it.

## Written to be quoted

If an AI answer appears almost every time, the website's job is to be the easiest source to quote. That shaped the writing as much as the design:

- **The answer comes first.** No three paragraphs of warm-up.
- **Formulas and tables are text, never pictures.** A picture cannot be quoted.
- **Every heading makes sense on its own**, so it still works when an assistant lifts it out of the page.
- **Nothing important hides behind a click.** The answers to frequently asked questions are in the page from the start, not loaded when someone opens them — because most AI tools read the page as it arrives, without clicking anything.

And the part everyone can check: Google's PageSpeed Insights gives the mobile site 100 for performance, accessibility, best practices and SEO, and 3 of 3 in its new test of how ready a site is for AI agents.

## Measuring before, so the after means something

On the day the case study went live, I asked ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google's AI overviews 74 questions a buyer might ask in this field. The new site was cited zero times.

That is exactly what you would expect for a site a few days old — and exactly why I measured it. Without a "before", a citation that shows up two months later cannot be attributed to anything. I will run the same 74 questions again in October and publish the comparison.

## What I would tell anyone commissioning a website now

**Check the results page before the design.** If an AI answer sits on top of the searches you care about, your pages need to be written to be quoted, not only to rank.

**Find where the volume really is.** For most specialists it is not in the name of their service. It is in the problems their clients try to solve on their own first.

**Be useful before the enquiry.** A calculator someone uses on Monday brings them back on Tuesday. A brochure does not.
